TL;DR: Staircases and inter-floor transitions add sloping structure that breaks the Manhattan world and makes most structural SLAM drift. Abstract

We present San Francisco SLAM (SF-SLAM), a structural RGB-D SLAM algorithm for accurate 6-DoF camera localization and mapping in geometrically complex structured environments. Existing visual SLAM methods often fail in textureless and repetitive scenes because feature scarcity and spatial ambiguity cause cumulative trajectory drift and tracking loss. These failures become more severe in staircases and inter-floor spaces, where repeated layouts and sloping structures fall outside the assumptions of the Manhattan world. To address these problems, we detect planes and lines to build a reliable global San Francisco world (SFW) map while simultaneously estimating 6-DoF camera poses, capturing structural regularities beyond orthogonal layouts. Anchoring orientation with structure-consistent absolute-rotation estimates removes rotation from the state and reduces planes and lines to 1-D invariant coordinates, yielding linear measurement models for camera translation and the global map. Our approach leverages both inter-floor plane and line regularities to preserve trajectory consistency in environments that violate the Manhattan world assumption. Our experiments in various SFW-structured scenes show that our approach effectively reduces the accumulated drift during inter-floor transitions and outperforms state-of-the-art SLAM methods in challenging real-world environments.

Method

The San Francisco world extends the Manhattan triad with four sloping dominant directions that share a common inclination, so the entire frame is fixed by just 4 degrees of freedom. SF-SLAM exploits this structure in three stages. A consensus-weighted rotation fusion combines the locally accurate VIO rotation with a drift-free SFW Visual Compass anchor, gated online by a long-baseline consensus test: inconsistent compass estimates are rejected outright (reverting to pure VIO), while accepted ones are down-weighted by their consensus score. MW-aligned planes are then refined to a single offset value via a 1-point RANSAC, while SFW-aligned lines are recovered through PCA on sampled 3-D points and classified as orthogonal or sloping. Because rotation is removed from the state and each structural constraint reduces to a 1-D invariant coordinate, the measurement models become strictly linear in the camera translation, yielding a single decoupled linear Kalman filter with no Jacobians and no accumulated linearization error.

The compass is an absolute reference, but it is not always trustworthy: a lone line parallel to the dominant plane adds no independent constraint, a vanishing-point assignment can be ambiguous, reflective surfaces and moving doors remove the tracked plane, and the estimated inclination can disagree across lines. Rather than gating each frame on its own residual — which cannot separate a fraction-of-a-degree bias from ordinary VIO noise — SF-SLAM scores every compass estimate against an anchor buffer of the seven most recent accepted frames, spaced at least 100 frames apart, so the agreement is always measured over a baseline of at least 600 frames.

SF-SLAM Dataset

General-purpose RGB-D benchmarks are largely confined to room-scale living-room and office scenes and do not include sustained inter-floor motion. Our SF-SLAM dataset addresses this gap by providing long, continuous RGB-D trajectories spanning multiple floors, with sustained motion through staircases and other sloped structures. We capture it using an iPhone 15 Pro Max with the Stray Scanner application across large-scale environments exhibiting diverse SFW structures, including staircases, ramps, and inter-floor transitions. The application records RGB-D sequences along with the corresponding ARKit trajectories. Table I summarizes the sequence statistics, where angular travel denotes the cumulative rotational motion over each sequence and is reported in units of 103 degrees. Our SF-SLAM dataset consists of two sequence types:

  • Closed-loop sequences: The camera returns to its initial pose, which anchors the accumulated drift measurement over long trajectories (leftmost column of Figure 3: the start and end views coincide).
  • Open-loop sequences: The camera climbs stairs and ends at a repeating structural corner several stories above the start point (rightmost column of Figure 3: the start and end views show the same corner on different stories).

Results

Across closed-loop and open-loop sequences, SF-SLAM achieves the lowest final drift error (FDE) on six of the seven sequences against recent baselines — HI-SLAM2, StructVIO, ORB-SLAM3, DROID-SLAM, and Planar-SLAM — without an explicit loop-closure module (the exception is the Parking Lot, where a baseline's loop closure snaps its endpoints together). The monocular baseline, HI-SLAM2, is aligned to the ARKit reference by Sim(3) Umeyama alignment (evo toolkit) and its FDE is computed with the recovered scale. In every table, best is bold and second-best is underlined; “–” marks tracking failure or no reported result.

Ablation study on the use of line landmarks

Plane landmarks are the base structural measurement of the linear formulation, so this ablation measures what line landmarks add on top of them (FDE in metres; lower is better). Adding line constraints reduces FDE on every sequence relative to the plane-only configuration. The effect is clearest on Academic Building A, where the plane-only configuration is in fact worse than VIO alone: the stairwell exposes few large planar surfaces within the effective depth range, so plane measurements arrive intermittently and occasional spurious associations perturb an otherwise smooth estimate. Adding line constraints removes this failure. Conversely, when line segments are short or fragmented, the estimator continues to rely primarily on planar landmarks.
